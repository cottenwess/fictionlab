Foundation

Isaac Asimov, 1951

Primary
200A Physical Sciences · 500A Social Sciences
Focused
501A Political Science

The Galactic Empire has stood for twelve thousand years and is about to fall, though almost no one can see it. Hari Seldon can, because he has built a mathematics for it. Psychohistory cannot predict what any single person will do, but across quadrillions of people it produces reliable law, and the law says the Empire is finished and thirty thousand years of barbarism are coming. Seldon cannot prevent the collapse. He can shorten the dark age to a single millennium by planting a colony of scholars at Terminus, on the galaxy's furthest edge, ostensibly to compile an encyclopedia. The book then follows that Foundation through its first century and a half and through a succession of crises, each one solved not by a hero but by a man who correctly reads the forces already in motion: a mayor who turns religion into an instrument of statecraft, a trader who does the same with commerce. Seldon himself returns only as a recording, appearing in a vault to confirm that the plan is holding.

The Fiction Laboratory pairs science fiction novels with structured reading packets for science and English classes. This is the companion page for this title: where to find the book, every source behind its packet with live links, and any correction made since the packet was printed.

Where to find the book

Borrow it free

  • Your public library. Search the catalog or ask at the desk. If they do not hold it, request it: interlibrary loan is free and usually takes a week. This is one of the most widely held science fiction novels in print, so it is rarely a difficult request.

  • Libby. libbyapp.com. Library ebooks and audiobooks on a phone or tablet, free with a library card. Several audiobook recordings circulate, including a full-cast dramatisation; any of them follows the same text.

  • Your school library. Very likely already there. If your school holds a science fiction shelf at all, this is usually on it.

Read free, legally

  • Not available. Foundation was published in 1951 and is still in copyright, so there is no free full text and there should not be one. Older titles in the Core Library are public domain and do link to Project Gutenberg and Open Library. Anything offering this book as a free download is pirating it.

Buy it

  • Bookshop.org. bookshop.org/9780008117498. Orders send the money to independent bookstores rather than to a warehouse. Any edition works: unlike some titles in this library, Foundation has one text and no revised version, so nothing in the packet depends on which printing you hold.

  • Your local bookshop. Any independent store can order it, usually within a few days, and it is inexpensive in paperback.

Bookshop.org links here are affiliate links. A purchase through one earns the Laboratory a small commission, paid by Bookshop out of its own margin and never added to your price. Borrowing is listed first for a reason, and it is still free.

Before you buy a boxed set

  • Read this one first, whatever the box says. Foundation is the first book written, but it is not first in the internal chronology, and several editions and boxed sets lead with the prequels Prelude to Foundation (1988) or Forward the Foundation (1993). Those were written nearly forty years later, they explain things this novel deliberately withholds, and starting there spoils the book. The packet covers the 1951 novel only.

Keep reading

  • The rest of the original trilogy. Foundation and Empire (1952) and Second Foundation (1953). The second book introduces a single individual who wrecks Seldon's plan, which is Asimov answering the biggest objection to his own premise, and it is worth reading for that alone.

Sources

12 in total

Everything in the packet is built on real research, and most of it is free. If a claim sparks a question, go to the source and check it yourself. That is the whole idea.

Start here

  • Turchin, Peter. "Psychohistory and Cliodynamics." Peter Turchin, 3 Sept. 2012, peterturchin.com/psychohistory-and-cliodynamics.

    The founder of the real field says both halves in one short post: the book influenced him, and Seldon's precision is impossible. Free, and shorter than this page.

What the book is built on, and what breaks it

  • Gibbon, Edward. History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire. Vol. 1, 1776. Project Gutenberg, www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/731.

    The template, and Asimov never hid it. Rome does not die in a battle; it thins from the centre out, which is exactly how the Galactic Empire fails. Free.

  • Lorenz, Edward N. "Deterministic Nonperiodic Flow." Journal of the Atmospheric Sciences, vol. 20, no. 2, 1963, pp. 130-41.

    The paper that ends psychohistory, published twelve years after the book. No randomness anywhere in it, and almost identical starting points still end up nowhere near each other.

  • Von Neumann, John, and Oskar Morgenstern. Theory of Games and Economic Behavior. Princeton UP, 1944.

    Published in the middle of the novel's magazine run. While Asimov was inventing a fictional mathematics of human behaviour, this was the real one arriving.

  • Turchin, Peter. "Arise 'Cliodynamics'." Nature, vol. 454, 2008, pp. 34-35.

    History studied with mathematics, argued in a first-rank science journal. What a real psychohistory looks like once the impossible parts are taken out.

Asimov, and where the book landed

  • Nevala-Lee, Alec. Astounding: John W. Campbell, Isaac Asimov, Robert A. Heinlein, L. Ron Hubbard, and the Golden Age of Science Fiction. Dey Street, 2018.

    How these stories were actually made, and by whom. The source for the editor who demanded a series instead of a story.

  • Nevala-Lee, Alec. "Asimov's Empire, Asimov's Wall." Public Books, 7 Jan. 2020, www.publicbooks.org/asimovs-empire-asimovs-wall.

    The record in full, mostly in Asimov's own published words, and the source for The Author section. Free to read.

  • Krugman, Paul. "Asimov's Foundation Novels Grounded My Economics." The Guardian, 4 Dec. 2012.

    A Nobel laureate on why this novel is the reason he became an economist. Adapted from his introduction to an edition of the trilogy.

Did not fit the printed page

  • Wiener, Norbert. Cybernetics: Or Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine. MIT Press, 1948.

    Published four years after the last part of Foundation ran, arguing that control and feedback obey common laws in machines, animals and societies. The introduction alone is worth it, and it is the closest the 1940s came to a general science of systems that includes people.

  • Quetelet, Adolphe. A Treatise on Man and the Development of His Faculties. 1835. Translated by R. Knox, William and Robert Chambers, 1842.

    Where the aggregate view of human beings starts, more than a century before Hari Seldon. Quetelet counted crime, marriage and mortality and found stable rates in populations that no individual could be said to obey. Old, free in scanned form, and the reason the packet refuses to credit Foundation with inventing statistical social science.

  • Durkheim, Émile. Suicide: A Study in Sociology. 1897. Translated by John A. Spaulding and George Simpson, Free Press, 1951.

    The demonstration that even the most private act imaginable has a stable, predictable rate across a population. Published the same year Wells was writing, and translated into English the same year Foundation was collected.

  • Lorenz, Edward N. "Deterministic Nonperiodic Flow." Journal of the Atmospheric Sciences, vol. 20, no. 2, 1963, pp. 130-41, journals.ametsoc.org.

    Listed on the printed page too, because it is the single most important item here. Free from the journal. Short, and the abstract alone carries the argument that ends psychohistory.
